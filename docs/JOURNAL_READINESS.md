### Direct data-source and rights check, 2026-11-22

The official [MOZ-Smishing dataset card](https://huggingface.co/datasets/MOZNLP/MOZ-Smishing) identifies the 2,561-record Portuguese corpus (552 smishing and 2,009 legitimate records) as `creativeml-openrail-m`. The ACL paper describes the data as released under an “open license,” but the dataset card's specific license identifier is the operative public metadata available in the repository. This license is commonly associated with model releases; its application to this message dataset and any underlying-message rights are not explained in the card. The public dataset preview also displays transaction codes and phone/name-like details in example legitimate messages; the paper says identifiers in legitimate messages were anonymized, but the visible examples indicate that residual sensitive fields require careful review. No data file was downloaded or analyzed. **Decision: do not use or redistribute this corpus for the project unless the authors clarify the intended license and a privacy review establishes that the records are appropriately redacted for the planned use.**

The [ACM IMC artifact repository](https://github.com/reportsmishing/Smishing-Dataset-IMC25) includes a CC BY 4.0 license file, and the paper describes redacting PII from message text and withholding raw phone numbers and complete URLs. This is clearer repository-level licensing evidence, but it does not by itself settle rights held by the people who originally posted the reports, privacy risks from message content, or whether the authors' ethics approval covers a new research use. Its data therefore remains a lead rather than an approved input. These are research-governance observations, not legal advice or a determination of rights.

This direct source check lowers confidence that either corpus can be adopted immediately for an undergraduate journal project. It does not change the recommendation to retain the current UCI work as a portfolio artifact and not claim journal readiness.

## Research direction after the updated scan

The prior candidate question was:

> How much do within-corpus cross-validation estimates differ from evaluation on an independently collected SMS corpus for fixed, lightweight text classifiers, after checking and removing cross-corpus duplicates?

Recent work now directly studies cross-dataset SMS spam/smishing evaluation, including lightweight classical models and UCI-to-Mendeley evaluation. Therefore, the question above is not recommended as the paper's contribution by itself. The literature scan still does not prove that every possible differentiated angle is exhausted, but it raises the novelty bar substantially.

The provenance/duplicate-audit idea has also been substantially covered by the 2026 IEEE Access review and empirical audit above, alongside the 2025 multi-dataset quality study. Do not use a basic cross-corpus duplication, leakage, or label-quality audit as the proposed contribution. A substantially different SMS question could still exist, but this preliminary scan has not identified one that is both clearly differentiated and feasible with verified public data.

The 2024 Super SMS dataset is one possible research lead, not an approved data source. The repository includes data folders, but the inspected repository materials do not state a data-specific license or reuse permission. The paper's own CC BY-NC-ND license does not, by itself, establish the dataset's independent reuse terms. Obtain a clear license or permission before using, adapting, or redistributing it. No messages have been sent to the maintainers, and no download or modeling on that dataset has been performed.

The 2024 NCSU SMS-phishing release is also not approved for this project at this time. Its repository publishes an MIT license, but the README describes raw messages collected from external gateways and includes destination numbers; campaign data also include URLs and timestamps. The license does not expressly resolve the underlying third-party content and privacy questions. In addition, the release is positive-only phishing data, so it could at most be considered later as a carefully governed sensitivity/recall challenge set, not as a standalone test for precision, F1, or a full confusion matrix. This review did not download or inspect message rows. No contact has been made with its authors.

The Mishra & Soni Mendeley record states CC BY 4.0 and describes three classes, but its own provenance notes make independence uncertain: the messages were converted from Internet images, labels were assigned from extraction sources, and the authors thank the UCI SMS Spam Collection contributors for making messages available. A future audit must compare exact and normalized texts against UCI and inspect the dataset's label/source mapping before any experimental use. CC BY 4.0 is recorded as the published dataset license; the provenance and possible overlap caveats remain research-validity concerns, not a claim that the license is unclear. During this review the public landing page was accessible, but the Mendeley file-list API returned HTTP 401 without authentication. No dataset files have been downloaded or used in modeling, so the duplicate audit remains pending access to the official archive.

## Gaps before journal submission

- Complete and document a reproducible literature search, inclusion criteria, and comparison table.
- Establish a research contribution that is distinct from prior benchmark, robustness, evasion, and dataset-quality studies.
- Verify explicit data licenses and provenance for every added corpus; obtain permission where the terms are unclear. NCSU and Super SMS reuse terms remain unresolved. The Mendeley candidate has an explicit CC BY 4.0 record, but provenance, label construction, UCI overlap, and access to its official archive must be resolved before use.
- If adding external data, audit exact and near duplicates across corpora before splitting or evaluation.
- Predefine the evaluation protocol before the final evaluation; report positive-class metrics, confusion matrices, appropriate uncertainty estimates, and error analysis.
- Keep the distinction between spam, smishing, and verified fraud explicit; only make claims supported by the chosen labels.
- Select a target journal only after the research question and contribution are settled, then follow its current author instructions.
- Complete author, funding, conflict-of-interest, contribution, ethics, and data-availability declarations truthfully.

Keep the current repository as a completed portfolio project; do not spend more time expanding experiments or formatting this draft for journal submission unless a supervisor identifies a specific, defensible contribution. ### Focused lockfile audit question (2026-11-22)

The next candidate is now bounded to one reported compatibility case: a pnpm 12 two-document `pnpm-lock.yaml` with `lockfileVersion: 9.0`, compared with a single-document control. pnpm's issue tracker reports that single-document consumers may read only the environment document and show zero dependencies; this is a public issue report, not a result reproduced by this project. [pnpm issue #13805](https://github.com/pnpm/pnpm/issues/13805)

### Candidate extension: instruction/data boundary in SMS triage (2026-11-22)

The prior screens already found generic cross-dataset SMS evaluation, duplicate/provenance audits, LLM explanations, and temporal/adversarial robustness too close to published work. A narrower candidate is to measure whether instruction-like content inside an SMS body can override an LLM-based triage prompt, and whether explicit untrusted-content boundaries reduce paired label flips while maintaining clean-set performance. The existing TF-IDF model would serve only as a non-instruction-following control.

This is a **candidate for further screening**, not a confirmed research gap. The 2026 *International Journal of Information Security* paper covers SMS LLMs, prompting, concept drift, and semantic-preserving adversarial edits; the targeted search did not find explicit prompt-injection language in its accessible description, but full-text/supplement review is still required. SafeBreach's report demonstrates notification-based indirect prompt injection via SMS against a voice assistant, which supports the practical relevance of the broader threat but does not establish novelty for an SMS classifier study.
